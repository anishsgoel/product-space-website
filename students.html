Fellowship & Projects

Twelve weeks of training, then a real client.

Every new member spends their first semester in the Product Fellowship, then joins a project team working directly with a client company on a real engagement.

What your first semester looks like

Fellowship

Twelve weeks of product management curriculum, running lecture and lab from late September through December.

Capstone

You are placed on a team in week two and build a product alongside the curriculum, presenting to the full membership and industry professionals in December.

General body meetings

The full club meets weekly for speaker events, case studies, and workshops.

Recruiting support

Support from senior members on learning about and preparing for the recruiting process.

Beyond the curriculum

The fellowship runs alongside the rest of club life rather than in place of it. Members sit weekly general body meetings, speaker events, and workshops through the semester.

We also run events with industry partners, including a product case competition hosted with Perplexity, where members work a real product problem end-to-end and present it.

What you'll cover

Twelve weeks, most of them pairing a lecture with a hands-on lab. The curriculum moves in order, from what the job is through discovery, business validation, design, and the metrics and technical fundamentals you need to work with engineers.

  1. 01

    Foundations

    What a product is, what a product manager actually does, cross-functional collaboration, and the product development life cycle.

  2. 02

    Methodology

    The move from waterfall to iterative work. Agile, Scrum, and Lean, with sprint planning and backlog management in Jira.

  3. 03

    Discovery

    User surveys and customer interviews, identifying real pain points, and building personas, stories, and user journeys.

  4. 04

    Business

    Market and competitive research, business outcome estimation, and deciding whether a thing is worth building at all.

  5. 05

    Design and build

    MVP scoping, prioritization frameworks, Figma wireframes and prototypes, and UI and UX principles.

  6. 06

    Metrics and technical

    KPIs and the HEART framework, A/B testing, APIs, and full stack and cloud fundamentals.

Alongside it you sit weekly labs, general body meetings, and speaker events, and you close the semester presenting your capstone. Total commitment is about four to six hours a week.

Every new member builds a product from scratch

Teams form in week two and build alongside the curriculum. Each team gets a mentor from the board. In December, teams present to the full membership and to industry professionals, and the strongest project could receive funding.

Recent capstones include Reverb, which synthesizes your calendar, email, and coursework into a daily briefing and prioritized action plan, delivered as a five-minute audio briefing or a skimmable one-pager; and Soundscape, which generates playlists from where you are, surfacing local artists and neighborhood music scenes as you move between cities.

  1. Weeks 2–3

    Team formation

    Teams form and select the problem they want to go after.

  2. Weeks 3–4

    Research

    User interviews and market research to validate the problem is real.

  3. Weeks 5–6

    Scoping

    MVP scoping, wireframes, and a written PRD.

  4. Weeks 7–9

    Build

    Prototype build and defining the metrics that count as success.

  5. Weeks 11–12

    Final pitch

    Presentation prep and the final pitch to industry professionals.

Prompting alone is table stakes

Product Space members are the most AI-capable builders on campus, and we teach several layers deeper than prompt writing. Members learn to direct AI, not just talk to it.

  • MCP servers and tool integrationWiring models into the systems they need to act on.
  • Agentic workflows and orchestrationDesigning multi-step work that runs without a human in every loop.
  • Evals and prompt architectureMeasuring whether the thing you built actually works.
  • Vibecoding with Claude Code and CursorShipping working software as a non-engineer.
  • Context engineeringGetting the right information in front of the model at the right time.
  • Model selection and cost tradeoffsChoosing what to run, and knowing what it costs you.

Project Teams

After the fellowship, you work on a real client.

Project teams are where the curriculum stops being theory. You join a small pod assigned to a company, own a piece of the work, and take it through a midterm and a final deliverable the client actually uses.

How an engagement works

Every client gets a dedicated team. Members join as product analysts and work directly on the engagement. Teams meet weekly with the client and present at both a midterm and a final review.

Analysts who lead well move up to senior product analyst and then project manager in later semesters, so the structure is a ladder, not a ceiling.

Project Manager ×1

Runs client communication, sets sprint timelines, and leads the engagement end-to-end.

Senior Product Analyst ×1

Leads execution, drives roadmap strategy, and reviews the team's work before it ships.

Product Analysts ×4–6

Execute data analytics, UI and UX design, and competitive market research.

Ten to twelve weeks, two deliverables

Engagements run on a structured lifecycle with pre-defined sprint milestones, a mid-semester deliverable review by our executive board, and bi-weekly client check-ins. Rapid iteration, with the quality gates left in.

  1. Weeks 1–4

    Scope

    Onboarding, initial research, and planning against the client's actual problem.

  2. Weeks 4–8

    Build

    Implementation and action, heading into the midterm deliverable.

  3. Midterm

    Review

    You present to the client and to our executive board, and take the notes.

  4. Weeks 8–11

    Iterate

    Revision against that feedback, then finalizing deliverables and findings.

  5. Week 12

    Final

    The final deliverable, handed to the client to actually use.

Business, tech, and design problems

Members are selected for strength across technical, design, and business domains, and teams are staffed so a project gets all three. What you work on depends on what the client actually needs.

Growth strategy

Building acquisition funnels, distribution and growth work, and monetization strategy.

Design

Problem validation, mockups and wireframes, user interviews, and design recommendations.

Marketing

Market research and segmentation, value proposition validation, and go-to-market strategy.

Development

Feature recommendation, data science and analytics, and competitive analysis.

What that looks like in practice

  1. Gemini-powered programmable search

    The team partnered with Google's Search Ads and Syndication group, working alongside their engineers to integrate Gemini into Programmable Search Engine.

    They analyzed query behavior, mapped the user journey for AI-assisted search, and delivered a PRD, a product roadmap, and Figma mockups built to Google's interface standards.

  2. Sizing the campus dining opportunity

    Uber asked whether the campus dining point-of-sale market was worth entering. The team ran stakeholder interviews with Georgetown Dining, Aramark, and Grubhub.

    They researched how campus dining systems are bought, run, and replaced, and returned a clear go or no-go recommendation grounded in what Uber's hardware could actually do.

  3. Positioning a new product

    GMAC needed to determine the product positioning for Advancery. The team ran competitive analysis and market research, leaning on Georgetown graduate students as primary sources.

    They identified target market preferences and handed over actionable next steps the client could take straight into their roadmap.

The Community

The part that isn't on the syllabus.

Product Space is a club before it is a consultancy. We go on a retreat every semester, throw parties for the days worth throwing parties for, give every project team a budget to go do something together, and play a truly unreasonable amount of Fish.

We build the club the same way we build products

The work is genuinely demanding, and the reason people stay is the people. Bonding is not an afterthought bolted onto the semester; it is budgeted, scheduled, and taken about as seriously as a client deadline.

Every project team gets its own budget for dinners, trips, and whatever else the team decides counts as team building. Beyond that, the whole club gets together weekly, usually over a card game that has caused more genuine conflict than any client engagement.

New members are folded in immediately. You are placed on a capstone team in week two and paired with a mentor from the board, so you have a small group before you have a big one.

A year in Product Space

Retreats, Georgetown Days, project team dinners, and an alarming number of card games.

Two weekends, the whole club

Twice a year, once each semester, the entire chapter goes away together for a weekend. There is a nominal schedule. It survives about four hours.

What actually happens is the thing that makes the rest of the year work: the new cohort stops being the new cohort, project teams that only knew each other over Zoom become friends, and somebody learns a card game on Friday night and spends all of Saturday trying to win it.
